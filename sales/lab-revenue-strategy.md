# 10XBeta Lab Revenue Strategy

> **Purpose:** Generate paying lab work from non-portco clients to de-risk studio revenue. The studio's lab capacity is a fixed asset; this workstream fills it with external client work, generating cash flow that doesn't require raising funds.
>
> **Companion to:** `sales/portco-capital-playbook.md` (portco capital strategy), `sales/SESSION_LOG.md` (session record).
>
> **Distinct from capital strategy:** The capital strategy raises money for portcos (equity in, dilution accepted). This strategy sells services (revenue in, no dilution). Same studio capabilities, different commercial mechanism.
>
> **Verification status:** University programs, VC funds, and corporate innovation arms named below are from training-data knowledge. Verify each is still active and still has the mandate described before initiating outreach.

---

## Why this workstream exists

10XBeta's lab is a fixed-cost asset. Engineers, equipment, lab tools, IP-filing capability, regulatory thinking, manufacturing relationships. That capability exists whether or not it's being used.

Three ways the studio fills it:

1. **Studio incubation** (in-house portcos) - the core studio activity. Equity capture mechanism.
2. **Portco follow-on work** - existing portcos buying lab time for specific phases of their development. Mixed mechanism (sometimes billed, sometimes treated as studio investment).
3. **External client work** - paying customers who are *not* portcos buying lab services. Pure revenue mechanism, no dilution, no equity capture.

This doc covers the third bucket. It's the one that de-risks the studio commercially: when external clients pay for lab work, the studio doesn't need to raise capital to fund its own operations.

**The four channels Frederick has identified for external client work:**

1. Universities with affiliated VC funds (de-risk their early-stage investments)
2. VCs investing in early-stage medtech (de-risk their portfolio companies)
3. Large medtech companies outsourcing development
4. Defense tech companies needing medical / hardware development

The common thread: each buyer needs medtech engineering execution and *isn't a portco we're incubating*. Selling capability, not equity.

## Channel 1: Universities with VC funds

### Target profile

Universities increasingly run affiliated VC funds (or have tight partnerships with funds) that invest in faculty / student / clinician spinouts. These spinouts typically have strong science but weak hardware engineering, regulatory pathway, IP filing, and manufacturing thinking. The university VC has an investment thesis but the team executing it lacks the capability set.

## Channel 2: VCs investing in early-stage medtech

### Target profile

Medtech VCs back companies whose founding teams are usually scientists or clinicians, not hardware engineers. After investing, the VC needs the portco to deliver a clinical-grade prototype, regulatory clarity, IP, and manufacturing pathway, often within 12-18 months. ## What makes 10XBeta different from traditional development firms

The competitive set in lab revenue is traditional medtech dev firms: Sagentia, Cambridge Consultants, TTP, Veranex, Velentium, Battelle, Smithwise. They're large, established, and slow. 10XBeta's differentiation has to be sharp because price competition with these firms is a losing game.

**The differentiation:**

1. **Platform OS productized R&D.** Opportunity Engine validates the problem. Lab tools build the POC. IP module files. This is a *system*, not just a team for hire.
2. **Studio velocity.** We ship in weeks because we built the studio model to ship in weeks.
3. **Operator mentality.** Our team has spun out portcos. We think like founders, not consultants.
4. **IP fluency.** We file, we read claims, we design around. Many dev firms send IP work to outside counsel and lose the engineering-IP feedback loop.
5. **Brooklyn Navy Yard location.** Lab tours land for NYC tech / finance / academic ecosystems. Physical credibility.
6. **Portfolio IP leverage.** We bring proprietary capabilities to client work that pure-services firms don't have access to.

Lead with these in every pitch. Price competition against Veranex is a losing game; differentiation on speed and productized capability is winnable.

## Engagement models across channels

**Fixed-scope project (most common):** SOW defines deliverables, milestones, payment schedule. Best for clients who know what they want. Used for ~70% of engagements typically.

**Time and materials:** day rate or hourly. Best for exploratory work or ongoing engagement with shifting scope. Used for ~20% typically.

**Equity-for-services:** 10XBeta takes equity in client company in lieu of full payment. Highly selective. Only when 10XBeta would invest as an angel anyway. Used for ~5% typically.

**Option-to-license:** 10XBeta develops on contract; client gets option to acquire resulting IP or company. Specialty arrangement for large medtech corporates. Used for ~5% typically.

Mix matters. Heavy fixed-scope keeps revenue predictable. T&M provides flexibility. Equity-for-services and option-to-license should be exceptions, not patterns.
